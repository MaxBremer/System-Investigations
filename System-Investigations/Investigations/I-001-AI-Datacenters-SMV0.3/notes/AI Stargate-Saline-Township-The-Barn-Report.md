# AI Stargate — Saline Township, Michigan (“The Barn”)

**As of:** August 19, 2026  
**Evidence convention:** **Disclosed** = stated by a project party or government record; **Registered** = contract, permit, application, or adjudicatory record; **Derived** = arithmetic from disclosed inputs; **Scenario** = sensitivity, not a forecast; **Unknown** = not publicly established.

## Bottom line

The Saline Township project is real, financed, and physically under construction. It is also much larger—and its public cost boundary much more complicated—than the original $4.8 billion local-development estimate suggests.

OpenAI describes a **1 GW compute campus**. DTE’s special contract is for **1,383 MW of utility demand**, backed by an unusually large **1,383 MW / 5.532 GWh battery portfolio**. Related Digital says it has closed **$16 billion of project financing** for the purpose-built Oracle campus. Oracle’s July 2026 Michigan industrial-facilities tax application values the development at approximately **$43.098 billion**, split between about **$9.9 billion of real property** and **$33.2 billion of personal property**. These are not additive figures: they represent different boundaries and dates.

The hard-number interpretation is:

- **$16.0 billion per disclosed GW of critical compute** for the financed real-estate/data-center project, or **$11.57 billion per contracted utility GW**.
- **$43.10 billion per compute GW** on the current tax-application basis, or **$31.16 billion per contracted utility GW**.
- The gap between $16 billion and $43.1 billion is consistent with a large tenant equipment layer—accelerators, servers, networking, and electrical/mechanical equipment—but public documents do not provide an audited reconciliation.
- The facility’s contractual power draw is not 1 GW. It is **1.383 GW**, implying roughly **10.9 TWh per year** at DTE’s modeled load factor—more electricity than many cities and some small states consume.
- Cooling is designed around a **closed-loop, air-cooled system**. The township’s enforceable documents estimate only **~20,000 gallons per day** of facility water use and prohibit high-water-use evaporative cooling. That is unusually low for a campus of this electrical scale, but construction dewatering and temporary batch-plant water are separate and should not be conflated with steady-state operation.

The principal uncertainties are no longer whether a project exists. They are execution, grid, and public-finance risks: delivery of the industrial substation, procurement and siting of 5.5 GWh of batteries, the pending Court of Appeals challenge to DTE’s approvals, and a contested 12-year, 50% property-tax abatement on a $43.1 billion application.

## 1. What is being built

The project occupies the west side of Saline Township, Washtenaw County. Related Digital assembled **eight properties totaling approximately 575 acres**. The consent-judgment and township materials place the active data-center development on roughly **250 acres**. Oracle and Related say the physical campus comprises **three single-story buildings of approximately 550,000 square feet each**, or roughly **1.65 million square feet** in total.

This is not a speculative rezoning shell. Oracle, OpenAI, Related Digital, Walbridge, and Michigan officials held a construction event on June 1, 2026. Related said all campus buildings were under active construction, with the first approaching completion; by then the site had logged more than **300,000 union labor hours** and employed more than **700 union tradespeople**. Aerial imagery published in late July showed a second large structure taking shape.

The campus is variously described using two scales:

1. **1 GW of compute capacity** — OpenAI’s project-level description.
2. **1,383 MW of DTE demand** — the legally relevant utility-service number.

These are compatible if the 1 GW figure is critical IT load and the balance is cooling, power conversion, storage losses, lighting, and other facility overhead. If so, the implied ratio is **1.383 facility MW per critical IT MW**, equivalent to an inferred full-load PUE of approximately **1.383**. This is only an inference: neither Oracle nor OpenAI has published a contracted PUE, a design PUE, or the exact meaning of “1 GW” for this site.

### Schedule and current state

The official project schedule calls for construction beginning in Q1 2026, first-building operation in **Q1 2027**, and phased construction continuing through 2027. DTE’s SEC disclosure says full contracted power delivery is expected in **December 2027**. Some public remarks describe full completion near the end of 2027 and broader operations in early 2028. The defensible reconciliation is that building turnover and compute energization will occur in phases; “operational” should not be read as the entire 1 GW campus commissioned on one date.

As of this report:

- **Civil and vertical construction:** underway.
- **First shell/building:** described as nearing completion in June.
- **Other buildings:** active construction visible.
- **Industrial substation:** procurement/construction path active, but township reporting noted equipment-delivery delay in June.
- **Compute equipment installation:** no public rack, accelerator, or commissioned-MW count.
- **Commercial operation:** no confirmed energized production capacity yet.

The project’s public schedule is aggressive but no longer purely aspirational. The critical path appears to be electrical infrastructure and equipment commissioning rather than entitlement.

## 2. Power: the enforceable structure beneath “grid benefits”

### 2.1 The service contract

In case **U-21990**, the Michigan Public Service Commission conditionally approved DTE Electric’s special power-supply agreement with **Green Chile Ventures LLC**, an Oracle subsidiary. The agreement is for **1,383 MW**, with a minimum term of approximately **19 years**.

The customer is subject to an **80% minimum billing demand**. If it terminates early, the contract provides for an exit payment covering as much as **ten years of minimum billing demand**. DTE testimony characterized the minimum recovery at approximately **$230 million per year for ten years**, or roughly **$2.3 billion**, even in a zero-load case. That is the core ratepayer-protection mechanism—not the project’s branding, tax projections, or employment promises.

The agreement also requires the data center to be curtailed before existing customers in an emergency. This helps address reliability during scarcity events, but it does not eliminate the need to plan energy, capacity, transmission, and distribution resources for normal operation.

### 2.2 Energy consumption

DTE’s testimony, as summarized in the MPSC order, places annual consumption at about **10.9 TWh**. A simple check:

\[
1.383\ \text{GW} \times 8{,}760\ \text{hours} \times 0.90 \approx 10.90\ \text{TWh/year}
\]

Relative to the 1.383 GW contract maximum, 10.9 TWh implies approximately 90% annual utilization. The same order refers to an **88.5% load-factor assumption** in the renewable-capacity calculation; the public summary does not reconcile that slight modeling difference. At 10.9 TWh, every $10/MWh change in effective energy cost corresponds to about **$109 million per year**. This illustrates why modest errors in fuel, market-price, or resource assumptions matter even with a strong minimum-bill provision.

### 2.3 Generation is not actually solved by saying “existing resources”

DTE says it can initially serve the load using existing generation, fuel, and purchased power. That is a near-term accounting statement, not a complete long-run supply plan. A 10.9 TWh/year increment changes renewable-compliance requirements, capacity needs, market exposure, and the dispatch of the existing fleet.

MPSC staff estimated that the new load would raise DTE’s renewable portfolio obligation by approximately **30.5%**. Without banked renewable-energy credits, compliance could require about **3.2 GW of solar**. With DTE’s banked credits and existing plan, testimony identified approximately **443 MW of additional renewable resources by 2032**. The Commission ordered DTE to compare alternatives in future integrated-resource and clean-energy planning.

Those figures are compliance scenarios, not a disclosed dedicated-generation plan for Oracle. No project-specific natural-gas plant, nuclear allocation, renewable PPA portfolio, or 24/7 carbon-matching structure has been published.

### 2.4 Storage: 1,383 MW for four hours

The energy-storage agreement is as large as the service contract: **1,383 MW of lithium-ion batteries with four-hour duration**, or **5.532 GWh**. DTE will develop, own, and operate the resources, with a stated portfolio target of approximately **65% utility-owned and 35% third-party-owned**. Green Chile pays the actual revenue requirement over **15 years**. Incremental MISO market revenue, net of charging and specified costs, accrues to the customer.

This is economically important. The storage is not a free grid amenity funded from project equity. It is a utility asset portfolio whose costs are contractually assigned to the data-center customer, subject to the approved agreement and ongoing litigation. DTE has described nearly **$2 billion** of storage investment over five years. Using that rounded figure:

- **~$1.45 billion per GW** of storage power capacity; and
- **~$361 per kWh** of four-hour energy capacity.

These are derived ratios, not vendor pricing. “Nearly $2 billion” may include development, interconnection, financing, and utility revenue-requirement items beyond battery modules.

### 2.5 Transmission and substation

Public disclosures identify approximately **$200 million of transmission upgrades** and a **$300 million industrial substation**, for a combined connection-infrastructure envelope of roughly **$500 million**. The landlord made a **$40 million nonrefundable construction advance**. Customer-responsibility provisions, contributions in aid of construction, and tariff recovery are intended to prevent these dedicated facilities from migrating to the general customer base.

On the 1.383 GW utility denominator, $500 million is approximately **$362 million per GW**. The $40 million advance should not be added mechanically; it is part of the funding arrangement rather than necessarily an extra cost layer.

The principal schedule warning is equipment delivery. Saline Township reported in June that delivery of substation equipment had slipped relative to the earlier mid-June construction expectation. No public record quantifies the resulting critical-path delay.

### 2.6 Litigation status

The utility approvals are not legally final in the practical sense. Michigan Attorney General Dana Nessel and environmental groups appealed the MPSC’s special-contract and battery orders. The Court of Appeals consolidated three related matters, and appellants filed briefs in August 2026. They argue, among other things, that the Commission improperly used ex parte procedures without a contested-case record adequate to test ratepayer and resource-planning risks.

The orders remain the governing approvals unless stayed or reversed; the report therefore treats the power structure as **approved but under active appellate challenge**.

Primary records: [MPSC approval release](https://www.michigan.gov/mpsc/commission/news-releases/2025/12/18/mpsc-approves-dte-electric-energy-contracts-for-data-center), [December 2025 order](https://mi-psc.my.site.com/sfc/servlet.shepherd/version/download/068cs00001XbIDsAAN), [DTE’s SEC contract disclosure](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/936340/000093634025000228/dte-20251028.htm), [Attorney General’s consolidated appellate brief](https://www.michigan.gov/ag/-/media/Project/Websites/AG/releases/2026/August/U-21990_AG_Appeal-Brief_380362_380364_380878.pdf), and [March 2026 battery order](https://mi-psc.my.site.com/sfc/servlet.shepherd/version/download/068cs00001rbvBcAAI).

## 3. Cooling, water, backup power, and environmental controls

### 3.1 Cooling architecture

The township consent judgment bars high-water-use evaporative cooling. Project materials describe a **closed-loop, air-cooled water/refrigerant system**: heat is rejected to air, while fluid circulates in a sealed loop. This is closer to a large air-cooled chiller/dry-cooler architecture than to an open cooling-tower system.

The anticipated steady-state facility water use is only **about 20,000 gallons per day**, for:

- domestic/restroom use;
- humidity control;
- landscaping;
- fire protection; and
- general maintenance.

At 1 GW of labeled compute, 20,000 gallons/day would equal only **0.02 gallons per kWh of IT capacity-hour if fully utilized**, before adjusting for actual load. That calculation is illustrative; a proper WUE requires metered annual water and IT energy, neither of which is public.

Cooling-system discharge may not be released onto the site or into waterways and must be removed in accordance with EGLE requirements. That implies a disposal/logistics obligation for maintenance drains and fluid replacement, even though daily make-up demand is low.

### 3.2 What the 20,000-gallon figure excludes

The operational number should not be used to dismiss all hydrological impact. Construction has required temporary wells, concrete-batch-plant water, grading, dewatering, and stormwater management. Those uses are metered or monitored under separate arrangements, and the township has published recurring well-monitoring updates. The public record does not yet provide a complete construction-period water balance.

Accordingly:

- **Steady-state cooling-water claim:** unusually specific and constrained.
- **Construction-period withdrawal:** incompletely quantified.
- **Aquifer impact:** no confirmed impairment in the material reviewed, but the monitoring period remains short.

### 3.3 Noise and emergency generators

The consent framework imposes a **55 dBA property-line limit** for operations and generator testing. Routine generator tests are restricted to daylight hours and no more than once per week. Emergency operation is treated separately.

Early permit materials referenced **14 diesel emergency generators plus one diesel fire-pump engine**. Later township advisories stated that generator sizing and updated information were under review. The initial count therefore should not be treated as a final campus-wide backup-power inventory. Missing data include generator MW, fuel storage, runtime, emissions totals at full build, and whether later phases add materially more units.

Primary local sources: [Saline Township project updates](https://salinetownship.org/data-center-update), [township FAQ and consent-summary PDF](https://salinetownship.org/go.php?id=731&table=page_uploads), and [official project site](https://www.thesalinebarn.com/).

## 4. Cost stack and per-GW normalization

### 4.1 Three cost boundaries—not one number

The project has generated three headline values:

1. **$4.811 billion** — the original 2025 consent-judgment estimate: $1.311B real property and $3.5B personal property.
2. **$16 billion** — Related Digital’s April 2026 closed financing for the purpose-built Oracle campus.
3. **$43.098 billion** — Oracle’s July 2026 industrial-facilities exemption application: approximately $9.9B real property and $33.2B personal property.

The $4.811 billion figure is superseded as a full-project estimate. The $16 billion financing likely captures land, buildings, electrical/mechanical plant, and other landlord/developer scope. The $43.098 billion tax basis appears to include a much larger tenant equipment layer. Public records do not reconcile each line item, and tax basis, project sources-and-uses, and replacement cost are not identical concepts.

### 4.2 Per-GW table

| Cost boundary | Total | Per 1 GW compute | Per 1.383 GW utility demand | What it likely covers |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Related project financing | $16.0B | $16.00B/GW | $11.57B/GW | Purpose-built Oracle campus; exact sources-and-uses undisclosed |
| Current IFEC application | $43.098B | $43.10B/GW | $31.16B/GW | Real + personal property for tax purposes |
| IFEC real property | ~$9.9B | ~$9.90B/GW | ~$7.16B/GW | Land improvements, buildings, fixed systems; exact schedule not public |
| IFEC personal property | ~$33.2B | ~$33.20B/GW | ~$24.01B/GW | Likely compute/network and movable electrical/mechanical equipment; not proven to be all GPU spend |
| Michigan contractor/supplier spend | >$5.0B | >$5.00B/GW | >$3.62B/GW | Geographic procurement subset, not an extra layer |
| Transmission + substation | ~$0.5B | ~$0.50B/GW | ~$0.362B/GW | Connection facilities; may sit inside broader project/utility budgets |
| Four-hour battery portfolio | nearly $2.0B | ~$2.0B/GW compute | ~$1.45B/GW utility | DTE five-year storage investment; customer-supported, exact final cost unknown |

**Do not sum this table.** It mixes overlapping scopes, owners, tax classifications, and financing periods.

### 4.3 What can be inferred from the personal-property number

The roughly **$33.2 billion** personal-property value is the strongest public clue to the compute stack. It is in the range one would expect for a 1 GW frontier-AI deployment after accelerators, host systems, networking, racks, power delivery, and cooling equipment are included. It should not be relabeled “GPU cost,” however. The tax schedule is not public at a line-item level, depreciation treatment is unknown, and some mechanical/electrical systems may be classified as personal property.

If the $9.9B real-property figure and $33.2B personal-property figure are directionally comparable, roughly **77%** of the current application value is personal property and **23%** is real property. That mix underscores why conventional data-center reporting focused only on buildings can miss most of the economic value.

### 4.4 Sensitivities

At the current application value:

- A 10% cost variance equals **$4.31 billion**.
- Each 100 MW of the 1 GW compute label represents about **$4.31 billion** of tax-application value if costs scale linearly.
- Each point of inferred PUE at this scale matters: reducing facility/IT ratio from 1.383 to 1.30 would lower full-load demand by about **83 MW**, before redundancy and operating margins.

These are arithmetic sensitivities, not project forecasts.

## 5. Financing and public support

### 5.1 Private financing

Related Digital announced on April 24, 2026 that it had secured **$16 billion** for the campus through Related and Blackstone-affiliated equity plus fixed-rate, long-term debt anchored by PIMCO. Bank of America structured the transaction and acted as financial adviser.

Financial reporting described approximately **$14 billion of 144A bonds**, about **$2 billion of equity**, a bond maturity in 2045, and a coupon near **7.5%**, with PIMCO taking roughly $10 billion. These are reported transaction terms rather than a published prospectus reviewed for this report; the primary announcement confirms the aggregate financing and capital types but not every allocation.

The structure is consistent with a lease-backed data-center financing: developer/financial-sponsor equity beneath long-dated fixed-rate debt, supported by Oracle tenancy/credit. The exact rent, lease duration, parent guarantee, debt-service coverage, completion support, and residual-value assumptions are not public.

Primary announcements: [Bank of America](https://newsroom.bankofamerica.com/content/newsroom/press-releases/2026/04/related-digital-announces-financing-for--16-billion-oracle-data-.html), [Blackstone](https://www.blackstone.com/news/press/related-digital-announces-financing-for-16-billion-oracle-data-center-project-in-saline-township-michigan/), and [Related](https://www.related.com/press-releases/2026-04-24/related-digital-announces-financing-16-billion-oracle-data-center-project).

### 5.2 Local property-tax abatement

Oracle sought a **12-year, 50% industrial-facilities property-tax abatement** on the current ~$43.1B application. The township first attempted to cap eligible value near the old $4.8B consent estimate, then reversed course after counsel warned that doing so conflicted with the 2025 settlement. The board subsequently approved the full application locally.

That does not make the benefit final. The State Tax Commission must issue the certificate. In August, an attorney challenged the process, arguing the township had not properly held the required public hearing for the underlying industrial-development district. The application is therefore best described as **locally approved, state-pending, and procedurally contested**.

The gross value of a 50% abatement cannot be calculated reliably from the $43.1B headline alone because taxable value, classification, depreciation, millage, timing of placed-in-service property, and state-program rules matter. Any simple “half of $43.1B” statement would be wrong: that would confuse asset value with tax savings.

Current reporting: [Planet Detroit on the uncapped approval](https://planetdetroit.org/2026/07/data-center-news-saline-township/) and [Michigan Advance on the procedural challenge](https://michiganadvance.com/2026/08/14/in-bid-to-stop-saline-data-center-tax-break-an-attorney-argues-the-township-skipped-crucial-steps/).

### 5.3 State sales-and-use-tax support

Michigan’s qualified data-center program can exempt eligible construction and equipment from the state’s **6% sales and use taxes**, subject to certification and statutory thresholds including at least **$250 million of capital investment**, at least **30 qualifying jobs**, wage requirements, and program conditions extending through 2050. The actual exempt purchase base for Saline has not been published.

Applying 6% to the entire $43.1B would overstate the subsidy because not all property or transactions are necessarily eligible or taxable at retail. The useful disclosure would be the certified exempt-purchase schedule; it is not public in the materials reviewed.

Program source: [Michigan Economic Development Corporation data-center program](https://www.michiganbusiness.org/services/data-center/).

### 5.4 Community payments and tax projections

Project parties identify **$14 million** for community, fire, and farmland-preservation measures, more than **$5 billion** of Michigan contractor/supplier work, and roughly **$1 billion of taxes over the lease term**. Oracle has also used language suggesting annual public revenue well north of $150 million for much of two decades. These claims use different tax bases and geographies and are forecasts, not collections.

No public fiscal model reviewed here reconciles:

- taxes paid under the local abatement;
- state sales/use-tax exemptions;
- school and county millages;
- depreciation of personal property;
- utility and income taxes; and
- one-time fees or community payments.

Until that reconciliation exists, the $1B and $150M/year claims should not be treated as interchangeable.

## 6. Jobs and local economic claims

OpenAI projects more than **2,500 union construction jobs**, **450 permanent onsite jobs**, **1,500 jobs across Washtenaw County**, and another **1,000 indirect jobs**. Related reported more than 700 tradespeople onsite by June.

The permanent-job hierarchy matters. “450 onsite” is the most concrete operational count. Countywide and indirect figures are economic-model outputs. At $43.1B of application value, the ratio is approximately **$95.8 million of asset value per projected permanent onsite job**. That is normal for extreme capital intensity but useful context when evaluating employment-based subsidies.

## 8. Known, inferred, and still missing

### Known or contractually registered

- 1,383 MW DTE demand.
- Minimum-term and minimum-bill/termination protections.
- 1,383 MW / 5.532 GWh storage obligation.
- Three ~550,000 sq ft buildings.
- Closed-loop air-cooled design and ~20,000 gpd anticipated operating water.
- $16B financing announcement.
- ~$43.098B local tax application.
- Physical construction underway.

### Reasonably inferred

- The 1 GW label is likely critical IT capacity, while 1.383 GW is gross facility demand.
- The $33.2B personal-property layer likely contains most compute and network equipment.
- Electrical delivery, not land-use entitlement, is the most important schedule constraint.

### Materially missing

- Accelerator type, count, vendor, and delivery cadence.
- Exact rack density and commissioned-MW ramp.
- Design and operating PUE/WUE.
- Tenant lease rent, term, guarantees, and completion covenants.
- Bond prospectus, debt-service profile, and equity waterfall.

## 9. Falsifiers and next records to obtain

This assessment should be revised if any of the following appears:

1. A tax schedule showing the $33.2B personal-property estimate excludes major compute hardware or materially double-counts equipment.
2. A lease or financing prospectus defines the 1 GW figure differently from critical IT load.
3. Court action stays or reverses the DTE special contracts or battery orders.
4. The State Tax Commission rejects, narrows, or conditions the industrial-facilities certificate.
5. A revised air permit shows a materially larger backup-generator fleet.
6. Metered water data exceed the 20,000 gpd operating estimate on a sustained basis after commissioning.

High-value records would include the IFEC asset schedule, DTE interconnection work orders, battery RFP awards, air-permit revisions, monthly construction-water reports, and any public financing offering memorandum.

## Assessment

Saline is one of the most documentable Stargate sites because its power contract, storage obligations, local settlement, tax application, and project financing expose multiple layers of the stack. The defensible headline is not simply “a $43 billion data center.” It is a **1 GW critical-compute / 1.383 GW utility campus**, financed at **$16B** on one boundary and currently valued at **$43.1B** for tax-application purposes on a broader boundary, with **5.532 GWh of customer-supported storage**, approximately **$500M of connection infrastructure**, low-water closed-loop cooling, and two live legal fronts.

The physical project is advanced enough that cancellation is no longer the base case. The more relevant question is whether electrical delivery, compute installation, and legal/tax execution remain synchronized with the Q1 2027 first-building target and the 2027–28 full-campus ramp.
