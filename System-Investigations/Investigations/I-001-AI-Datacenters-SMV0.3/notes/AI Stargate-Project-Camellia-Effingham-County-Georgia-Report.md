# Stargate — Project Camellia, Effingham County, Georgia

**As of September 2, 2026**

## Bottom line

Project Camellia is best classified as an **OpenAI-led branch of Stargate, but not an Oracle-led Stargate campus**. OpenAI's announcement page is filed under its Stargate category, and OpenAI is the named designer, developer, utility customer and ultimate compute user. Oracle, SoftBank and SB Energy have no disclosed role. OpenAI is still selecting a construction/operations partner and seeking development and financing partners, so “OpenAI-led” does not yet mean OpenAI will own every physical layer.

The two headline numbers need correction:

- **The defensible capacity number is 3.2 GW of utility service, not 3 GW of confirmed IT load.** Georgia Power's contract covers 3,200 MW; the state development filing gives a 3,210 MW **peak connected electrical load**. Neither source discloses critical IT load or design PUE.
- **The defensible committed investment floor is $20 billion, not $30 billion.** The state filing lists a $20 billion build-out value, and the local agreement reportedly requires five $4 billion investment phases. OpenAI compute executive Sachin Katti said a full 3.2 GW build would “probably” cost more than $30 billion. That is a management estimate, not a closed financing, itemized budget or legal commitment.

Normalized against utility load, the two figures are only **$6.23B/GW** and **more than $9.35B/GW**. Even after converting the denominator to a plausible IT-load range, the greater-than-$30B estimate lands around **$10.8B–$12.2B per implied IT GW**—near the low end of the existing powered-campus range and far below an all-in campus plus GPU stack. The public numbers therefore cannot be read as the cost of a fully equipped 3.2 IT-GW AI system.

Camellia does not overturn the consolidated capital-stack model. It sharpens it. The important new role is **design and procurement control**: OpenAI can control the campus design, sign the utility contract and remain the demand anchor while still outsourcing construction, operations and much of the asset financing. The model should distinguish that control layer from legal ownership.

The project is materially more advanced than a press release because its power contract cleared regulatory review on August 26 and its Development of Regional Impact process is marked complete. It is nevertheless **pre-construction, unfinanced in public, unpermitted at the detailed site level and zero-GW operational**. Initial capacity is targeted for 2028; full build-out remains conditional.

## Evidence labels

- **Disclosed:** stated by OpenAI, Georgia Power, a government body or a public filing.
- **Contracted/committed:** supported by an executed or regulator-reviewed agreement, although confidential terms may remain unknown.
- **Reported:** credible reporting based on interviews or obtained documents.
- **Derived:** arithmetic from disclosed, contracted or reported inputs.
- **Scenario:** explicit comparison assumption, not a project forecast.
- **Unknown:** no reliable public figure found.

## Is Camellia part of Stargate?

**Yes in the broader OpenAI program; no in the narrow Oracle implementation model.**

OpenAI's July 22 Camellia announcement is categorized under **Stargate** on OpenAI's website. The body of the announcement does not name Oracle, SoftBank or the original Stargate joint venture. Instead, OpenAI says it is “designing and developing” the project itself. Its project FAQ says OpenAI is running a competitive process to select a partner to construct and operate the campus.

That supports a two-level definition:

1. **Stargate as program:** OpenAI's umbrella for securing very large blocks of AI infrastructure and compute.
2. **Stargate as a specific commercial chain:** Oracle leases a campus, owns/finances the compute and sells OCI capacity to OpenAI.

Camellia belongs to the first category but does not currently fit the second. The project is evidence that “Stargate” should not be modeled as synonymous with Oracle or with a single joint venture.

Sources: [OpenAI announcement and Stargate category](https://openai.com/index/building-ai-infrastructure-with-the-effingham-county-community/) and [Project Camellia FAQ](https://projectcamellia.com/faq).

## Site, buildings and schedule

The project is planned inside the **Savannah Gateway Industrial Hub** near Rincon in unincorporated Effingham County. The Effingham County Industrial Development Authority owns the industrial-park property; Broe Real Estate/OmniTRAX is the master developer and controlled negotiations over the relevant parcels. The Camellia tract is approximately 1,400–1,440 acres, not the full 2,600-acre hub.

The state DRI filing identifies:

- four buildings;
- 4.4 million square feet total;
- a 3,210 MW peak connected load;
- a 2032 overall completion target;
- 30,000 gallons/day of water and the same amount of sewage;
- 65% impervious coverage; and
- permits, including certificates of occupancy and tree-removal/business approvals, as the local action requested.

The filing is useful but visibly incomplete. It leaves boxes and descriptions blank for water-line capacity, sewer expansion, transmission sufficiency, on-site generation, wetlands, floodplains and other sensitive resources. It says only that applicable studies and permits will be followed. A completed DRI is therefore **not a construction permit or environmental clearance**; Georgia describes the DRI process as an advisory intergovernmental review.

There are also two material internal inconsistencies:

- The DRI says **4.4M square feet**, while a county project site says four buildings of about **720,000 square feet each**, or **2.88M square feet**. The 1.52M-square-foot difference is too large to ignore.
- OpenAI and the DRI target full capacity/build-out by **2032**, while reporting on the local MOU describes five construction phases running from **2028 through 2034**.

These may reflect evolving design, different definitions of floor area or tax phases that continue after power delivery. Until a reconciled site plan is approved, neither discrepancy should be “fixed” by choosing the more convenient figure.

The DCA docket now marks DRI #4801 **Completed**, with an RDC finding dated September 1. Local reporting says the next step is preliminary site-plan review, expected for a county vote on September 14, followed by detailed review of wetlands, stormwater, land disturbance and other permits. No vertical construction or operational capacity is publicly confirmed.

## Power: the strongest part of the project record

The power arrangement is Camellia's most concrete asset.

Georgia Power and OpenAI have a **25-year agreement for approximately 3,200 MW**, phased from 2028 through 2032. OpenAI says it will pay the full infrastructure and electric-service costs required to serve the project and provide financial assurances under Georgia's large-load rules. On August 26, Georgia PSC staff allowed the contract to clear review after adding protections and transparency commitments. The full contract remains redacted.

The disclosed risk-transfer provisions include:

- OpenAI funds the dedicated infrastructure and pays the cost of service;
- the contract must include long-term commitments and financial assurances;
- up to **1,000 MW** can be curtailed during specified high-demand/grid-stress periods;
- Georgia Power agreed not to recover revenue deficiencies from residential and small-business customers if a large-load customer terminates early;
- Georgia Power must publish a summary of this contract and semiannual public reports on its large-load data-center portfolio.

The public record still withholds the economic terms that matter most to project finance: the demand ramp, tariff, minimum monthly payments, take-or-pay obligation, collateral amount, termination schedule, infrastructure budget, fuel adjustment, curtailment notice/duration and damages. Regulatory clearance proves that staff reviewed a real contract; it does not make those terms auditable.

### Capacity scale check

At a 90% average load factor, 3.21 GW would consume approximately **25.3 TWh/year**. Georgia Power reported 89.3 TWh of territorial sales in 2025, so a fully loaded Camellia would equal about **28% of the utility's 2025 territorial electricity sales**. This is a scale comparison, not an operating forecast.

The 1 GW flexible-load commitment is economically important: it lets Georgia Power plan around a smaller firm peak than the full nameplate under some conditions. But it does not turn the whole campus into interruptible load. Roughly 2.2 GW remains outside the disclosed flexibility ceiling, and the duration/frequency of curtailment is unknown.

### Generation and transmission

No project-specific generation mix or cost has been disclosed. The site expects grid service and only backup diesel generation on site; regular generation will be off site. Georgia Power says new substations and transmission will be needed, but it has not published Camellia's dedicated grid budget or route.

Georgia Power is simultaneously pursuing a much larger portfolio expansion—roughly 9.9–10 GW of additional capacity, transmission work and natural-gas plant expansions for statewide large-load growth. Those assets may serve Camellia, but the **$16.3B statewide construction plan cannot be allocated pro rata to this one campus**. Doing so would mistake a portfolio resource plan for a site cost stack.

## Cooling, water and environmental constraints

OpenAI says Camellia will use a **closed-loop cooling-water system**. Water recirculates rather than being routinely consumed through evaporative cooling. The DRI's **30,000 gallons/day** is described as recurring workplace, cleaning and maintenance demand—not the one-time initial system fill. The fill volume, makeup-water requirement, design WUE, equipment configuration and final annual demand remain unknown.

County material says the project will use **treated surface water rather than groundwater from the Floridan aquifer** and that a future water/sewer agreement will set quantities and protections. That matters because the site lies in a restricted groundwater-use area. The promise is directionally credible but not yet a complete engineering water budget.

Low cooling-water consumption does not remove the main environmental site risk. At 65% impervious coverage across roughly 1,400 acres, the developed surface could approach **900 acres** depending on the final tract definition. The regional review flagged wetlands, floodplains, drainage, stormwater, wildlife and water quality for further work. Final site layout and mitigation are not public.

Sources: [OpenAI announcement](https://openai.com/index/building-ai-infrastructure-with-the-effingham-county-community/), [Project Camellia technical FAQ](https://projectcamellia.com/faq), [DCA additional form](https://apps.dca.ga.gov/DRI/AdditionalForm.aspx?driid=4801), [Effingham County water summary](https://www.effinghamcounty.org/902/Project-Camellia-OpenAI-Data-Center), and [final-review reporting](https://www.statesboroherald.com/local/georgia-power-openai-contract-approved-for-effingham-center/).

The absence of Oracle is real, but the absence of an intermediary is not yet proven. OpenAI may ultimately select a Vantage-, Crusoe-, Related- or SB Energy-like owner/operator and place campus debt at an SPV. Camellia currently removes Oracle from the disclosed chain; it does **not** demonstrate a wholly OpenAI-owned balance-sheet build.

The public financing record is extremely thin:

- no project debt has been announced;
- no lender, bond amount, coupon, maturity or equity commitment has been disclosed;
- no construction or operating partner has been selected publicly;
- no GPU purchase, lease or residual-value guarantee has been disclosed;
- the confidential Georgia Power contract contains financial assurances, but their amount and provider are unknown.

This means the project has a credible demand sponsor, site path and utility contract, but **not a publicly closed end-to-end capital stack**.

## Tax and community-benefit accounting

The local agreement reportedly commits Octans/OpenAI to **$4B and 80 jobs in each of five phases**, producing the $20B/400-job floor. In return, the project receives a 15-year property-tax incentive for each phase. Public descriptions call it a **50% abatement**, while one report describes a formula assessing property at 25% rather than Georgia's ordinary 40% of fair market value—mathematically a 37.5% reduction before considering other terms—and freezing the 2026 millage rate. An open-records analysis estimates roughly **$870M paid and a similar amount forgone** over 15 years. These descriptions have not been reconciled in a public final PILOT schedule.

The DRI separately gives **$20M of annual local tax revenue**, while county projections show property-tax revenue ramping with construction and more than **$43M/year of local sales-tax revenue by 2032**. These are different tax streams and projection dates. They should not be added into a single guaranteed benefit figure.

The $80M community commitment is a real additional program but lacks a final annual disbursement schedule and governance framework. The $71M Codex-credit figure is the face value of a product benefit—$100 for approximately 721,000 eligible students—not cash paid to governments or residents. Neither belongs in construction capex.

One useful scale check: the binding 400-job floor equals **$50M of committed capital per permanent job**. Even the projected 1,000-job full build would equal at least $20M per job at the $20B floor. That is normal for an extraordinarily capital-intensive data center, but it shows why job counts should not be the principal economic justification.

Sources: [The Current MOU investigation](https://thecurrentga.org/2026/08/08/anatomy-of-a-secret-coastal-georgia-data-center-deal/), [WTOC tax-terms reporting](https://www.wtoc.com/2026/07/30/records-show-effingham-county-leaders-finalized-massive-tax-break-20b-openai-data-center-day-before-public-announcement/), [DCA additional form](https://apps.dca.ga.gov/DRI/AdditionalForm.aspx?driid=4801), [Effingham County sales-tax projections](https://www.effinghamcounty.org/902/Project-Camellia-OpenAI-Data-Center), and [OpenAI community commitments](https://openai.com/index/building-ai-infrastructure-with-the-effingham-county-community/).

## Cost-stack reconciliation

### What the two capex figures imply

The denominator for the following first calculation is the DRI's **3.210 GW peak connected electrical load**.

| Cost figure | Site total | Per utility GW | Evidence | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| DRI/MOU floor | $20B | **$6.23B/GW** | Filed/contracted floor + derived | Incentive-qualified build-out value; boundary unitemized |
| Full-build management estimate | >$30B | **>$9.35B/GW** | Reported estimate + derived | Not a budget or financing close |
| Land/site | Unknown | Unknown | Unknown | Land-sale proceeds exist, but price and sitework are not disclosed |
| Shell + MEP | Unknown | Unknown | Unknown | Four-building scope exists; no construction budget |
| Utility power infrastructure | Unknown | Unknown | Contracted obligation, no amount | Georgia Power assets paid through OpenAI's service arrangement |
| GPU + networking | Unknown | Unknown | Unknown | No hardware owner, vendor package, count or budget |
| Financing cost | Unknown | Unknown | Unknown | No campus debt terms; utility collateral amount confidential |

Because 3.210 GW is facility/grid load, the IT denominator must be smaller. With no disclosed PUE, a **scenario** using PUE 1.15–1.30 implies approximately **2.47–2.79 IT GW**. On that denominator:

- $20B equals approximately **$7.2B–$8.1B per implied IT GW**.
- More than $30B equals **more than $10.8B–$12.2B per implied IT GW**.

The consolidated Stargate work found powered-campus layers clustering around **$12.5B–$18.5B per IT GW** and an Abilene current-generation compute proxy near **$33.3B/IT GW**, for roughly **$46B–$52B/IT GW all-in**. Camellia's greater-than-$30B estimate is therefore numerically plausible as a low-end **facility/common-infrastructure envelope**, but not as a fully equipped current-generation AI campus.

Applying the prior all-in range mechanically to 2.47–2.79 implied IT GW produces roughly **$114B–$145B**. This is not a Camellia forecast. It shows how much hardware scope, future price change, phased utilization or denominator mismatch would be required to reconcile an all-in reading of the $30B headline.

### What can and cannot be concluded

**Supported:** the public $20B and greater-than-$30B figures are much closer to a campus/facility number than to the current consolidated all-in model.

**Not supported:** a claim that Camellia proves AI campuses can be fully built and equipped for about $9B/GW.

At least four explanations may coexist:

1. the $20B is an incentive floor rather than the economic total;
2. the greater-than-$30B estimate excludes some or all compute hardware and utility assets;
3. the 3.2 GW utility reservation will be energized or equipped gradually rather than filled with day-one compute; and
4. future hardware may deliver more compute per watt and per dollar than the Abilene generation.

The public record cannot yet choose among them or quantify their shares.

## How Camellia aligns with the current model

### Confirmed

1. **Stargate is an umbrella, not one capital stack.** Camellia is categorized by OpenAI as Stargate but uses a different commercial chain from Abilene.
2. **Power contracts precede buildings.** A 25-year, regulator-reviewed service agreement exists before final site plan, detailed permits, partner selection or project financing.
3. **The ultimate demand risk still lands at OpenAI.** Here it is unusually explicit because OpenAI—not Oracle—is the utility counterparty, developer lead and compute user.
4. **Utility assets remain a separate ownership layer.** Georgia Power will build/own regulated generation, transmission and substations; OpenAI supports repayment through service payments and financial assurances.
5. **Headline capex is boundary-sensitive.** The $20B filing value, greater-than-$30B estimate and unknown GPU layer cannot be treated as one interchangeable number.
6. **Public incentives are risk-sharing, not free capital.** The land/PILOT structure reduces local tax cost; it does not demonstrate that ECIDA funds the data-center build.

### Extended or revised

1. **Add a “design/control” layer.** OpenAI can dictate architecture and procurement while a future SPV or partner may own and finance the facility. Control, title and debt should be separate columns.
2. **Replace “Oracle usually anchors the lease” with branch logic.** Oracle remains one major route; direct OpenAI utility/development contracts are now a demonstrated alternative.
3. **Model flexible load as part of the power contract.** Camellia's 1 GW curtailment right reduces the utility's firm-capacity obligation and changes the value of the power asset. It is not merely an operational footnote.
4. **Treat regulatory/contract milestones independently.** Utility contract approval, DRI completion, site-plan approval, detailed permits, financing close, construction start and energization are separate gates. Camellia is advanced at the first two and incomplete at the rest.
5. **Make political legitimacy a delivery constraint.** The project obtained its MOU after confidential negotiations and announced the community process afterward. The subsequent backlash, calls for a moratorium and new disclosure commitments show that local consent and transparency can affect schedule and financing even when land and power are available.

## New insights for the wider investigation

### 1. OpenAI is experimenting with vertical coordination, not necessarily vertical ownership

Camellia is OpenAI's clearest attempt to become the system architect: choose the site, lead design, contract directly for power, select the builder/operator and possibly operate part of the campus. But OpenAI is still seeking partners. The likely economic gain is reduced coordination friction and a facility optimized for OpenAI workloads, not necessarily putting every asset on OpenAI's balance sheet.

### 2. The utility contract may be the true anchor contract

In the Oracle model, a long facility lease makes project debt financeable. At Camellia, the earliest hard commitment is OpenAI's 25-year utility contract. That contract supports billions of regulated utility investment before the building capital stack is public. For giga-campus analysis, the electricity-service agreement can be as consequential as the real-estate lease.

### 3. Demand response is becoming financing architecture

The right to shed up to 1 GW reduces the amount of always-available capacity Georgia Power must build. In effect, OpenAI is paying partly with operational flexibility. Future site models should record firm load, interruptible/flexible load, response duration and compensation separately.

### 4. The $30B headline reinforces the “missing GPUs” diagnostic

Whenever a multi-GW AI campus is announced at under roughly $15B/GW without a clear IT denominator and equipment schedule, the first presumption should be **incomplete scope**, not an infrastructure cost breakthrough. Camellia is a clean example: the public estimate is below the existing compute-hardware proxy before land, buildings or power are added.

### 5. Capacity reservation is not capacity delivery

Camellia has a real contracted ramp, but Georgia Power still must plan and build substations, transmission and generation. The 2028–2032 schedule depends on both a utility portfolio build and a yet-unselected campus partner. “3.2 GW secured” is stronger than a speculative interconnection request but weaker than funded, permitted and energized capacity.

## Current classification

The strongest defensible status is:

**Stargate-affiliated OpenAI-led project; site and utility contracts materially advanced; DRI complete; detailed site approval, permits, builder/operator selection and project financing outstanding; initial 2028 power target; zero operational capacity.**

## Highest-value follow-up evidence

1. Georgia Power's promised public contract summary, especially minimum payments, collateral, termination liability and load-ramp schedule.
2. The September preliminary site-plan package and vote, which should resolve building area, layout, buffers and phase definitions.
3. Final land-sale, bond-for-title/PILOT and lease documents, including parcel price and recapture provisions.
4. Selection of construction, operating and financing partners.
5. Project debt/equity financing close and a sources-and-uses schedule.
6. Critical IT load, design PUE, rack density and equipment architecture.
7. GPU/network vendor, buyer, financing structure and refresh plan.
8. Camellia-specific Georgia Power generation, transmission and substation assets and costs.
9. Final water/sewer agreement, initial-fill volume, ongoing WUE and discharge plan.
10. Evidence of grading, building permits, construction start and first energized capacity.
